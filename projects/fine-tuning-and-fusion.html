Fine-tuning and fusion

Towards accurate protein–peptide binding-affinity prediction. My B.Sc. Physics thesis at the Chair of Theoretical Biophysics, Technical University of Munich, supervised by Prof. Dr. Martin Zacharias and advised by M.Sc. Niklas Halbwedl.

All research & projects

Three questions

  1. Question 1

    Does Boltz-2’s affinity head transfer to peptides?

    No. It reaches r = 0.30 at best, and that signal largely tracks molecular weight.

  2. Question 2

    Does fine-tuning help, and do four architectures learn the same thing?

    It helps every model, lifting three of them to about r = 0.44. Yet they learn different signals and correlate only weakly with each other.

  3. Question 3

    Does combining them beat any single model?

    Yes. A de-biased mean of all four reaches r = 0.573, with a mean absolute error of 1.46 kcal/mol.

The problem

Cyclic peptides are large enough to engage protein surfaces that small molecules cannot reach, yet can stay cell-permeable. That makes them attractive drugs, and hard to model.

Boltz-2 predicts the structure of biomolecular complexes and, through a separate head, their binding affinity. That affinity head was trained only on protein–small-molecule data, so peptides sit outside its training distribution. It also accepts the binder only as a SMILES string, a text notation for small molecules.

Structure first

Encoding the peptide as SMILES hurt structure prediction badly. The median centroid distance to the crystal pose was 3.56 Å, against 0.74 Å when the peptide was encoded as a chain of residues. To keep the better structure and still use the SMILES-only affinity head, I built Boltzina, a fork of Boltz-2 that skips the structure module and injects externally computed coordinates. It runs 7.3 to 11.8 times faster.

Then affinity

Untuned, the affinity head reached r ≈ 0.30 at best, well below the r ≈ 0.62 Boltz-2 reports for small-molecule ligands, and the signal mostly tracked molecular weight.

I fine-tuned the head under both encodings. On SMILES input, test-set r rose from 0.257 to 0.438. On residue input it rose from 0.059 to 0.431. Alongside, I fine-tuned a graph neural network (GEMS, r = 0.431) and a protein language model (r = 0.340) on the same receptor-disjoint split of about 3,000 complexes.

The four models correlate only weakly with each other, even the two that share an architecture. Averaging their de-biased predictions gave r = 0.573, with a mean absolute error of 1.46 kcal/mol, better than any single model and better than fusing the untuned versions (r = 0.435). The gain holds after controlling for peptide size and interface residue count.

Fine-tuning and fusion each help on their own, and more so together.

Combining several independently fine-tuned models looks like a cheaper alternative to designing and training one large, purpose-built model from scratch.

One fused score

0.573

The four fine-tuned models, averaged, beat every one of them alone. The test set is receptor-disjoint at 90 % sequence identity and spans far more receptor families than the training data, so the number measures generalization rather than recall.

Fine-tuning and combining existing models could be a cheaper route than training one large purpose-built model from scratch.
